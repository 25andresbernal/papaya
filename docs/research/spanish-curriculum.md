# Spanish Curriculum Research — Papaya

**Prepared for:** Papaya, a gamified Spanish-learning web app for kids ages 5–10 growing up in English-speaking homes with one Colombian Spanish-speaking parent.
**Goal of this research:** ground Papaya's word order, dialect choices, and exercise design in how young children actually acquire a second language, in how comparable apps (Duolingo, Duolingo ABC, Lingokids) sequence content, and in what "useful household Spanish" looks like for a kid talking to a Colombian parent and grandparents.
**Date:** 2026-09-26

---

## 1. How young children acquire vocabulary, and what the first 300–500 words should be

### 1.1 First-language research as the model for word *order*, not word *speed*

The best evidence for "what words come first" comes from first-language acquisition research (Katherine Nelson's classic study of children's first 50 words), because it maps directly onto what a child actually needs to talk about at home. Nelson's categories, in order of dominance, were:

- **General nominals** (things/categories — ball, dog, milk) — the largest share, about half of a 50-word vocabulary.
- **Specific nominals** (names of unique people/pets — Mommy, Daddy, the family dog's name).
- **Action words** (verbs — go, up, more, eat).
- **Personal-social words** (yes, no, please, bye-bye).
- **Modifiers** (hot, big, dirty).
- **Grammatical function words** (this, that, for) — smallest share, come last.

First nouns are overwhelmingly concrete and touchable: people, food, animals, clothes, toys, body parts. Relational words (verbs, adjectives) make up only about a third of the earliest vocabulary. This matches the classic "nouns before verbs before function words/grammar" pattern seen across languages.

For **second-language acquisition specifically**, the order is far less fixed than in first-language acquisition — it's shaped heavily by what the child is actually asked to do and hear ("first-language interference or general cognitive interference" makes order less predictable), and unlike L1 learners, L2 learners need **direct, intentional instruction** rather than passive absorption, and the runway from single words to two-word combinations is roughly half as long (about 12 months vs. 24). Practically, this means: (a) start with the same concrete-noun-and-command backbone that L1 research supports, because it's what the child will actually be able to use immediately with a parent, and (b) don't wait for "natural" grammar to show up — sequence deliberately, and lean on repetition and short phrases rather than expecting the child to self-discover structure.

### 1.2 What this means for Papaya's first 300–500 words

Combining Nelson's categories with the specific ask (a kid talking to a Colombian dad and grandparents) gives a natural priority order:

1. **Greetings & routine social phrases** (hola, adiós, buenos días, gracias, por favor) — used every single interaction, highest payoff per word learned.
2. **Family & people** (mamá, papá, hermano/a, abuela/o, familia) — who the child is actually talking to and about.
3. **Feelings/state phrases** (feliz, triste, tengo hambre, tengo sueño) — lets the child self-report, which is what a parent most wants from a young child.
4. **Body** (needed for daily-care commands: lávate las manos, cepíllate los dientes).
5. **Food** (needed for mealtime, which is a daily, high-frequency ritual in Latin American households).
6. **Colors, numbers, animals, clothes, home, school** — concrete, high-frequency noun sets, easy to picture, natural fit for picture-based exercises.
7. **Action verbs** (correr, comer, jugar, dormir) — needed to build the first two/three-word combinations.
8. **Question words & polite phrases** (¿qué?, ¿dónde?, por favor, gracias, perdón) — needed once the child starts producing rather than only receiving.
9. **Parent commands** (ven aquí, lávate las manos, a comer, silencio, buenas noches, te quiero) — these are not "vocabulary" in the traditional sense, but they are some of the *highest-frequency input* a young child receives (TPR research estimates as much as a third to a half of all speech a young child hears is in the form of commands — see §5), so they deserve early, explicit teaching even though they are full phrases rather than single words.

This ordering is the backbone of the Section 6 curriculum below.

## 2. How Duolingo, Duolingo ABC, and Lingokids sequence content

### 2.1 Duolingo (Spanish from English) — first units

Duolingo's tree opens with a unit literally called **"Form basic sentences, greet people."** Unit 1 ("Intro") is three lessons on gender/articles, people, and eating/drinking, plus a review lesson — teaching **el/la, soy/eres, greetings, and basic food nouns** together from day one (Duolingo does *not* wait to introduce grammatical gender; it's baked into the very first nouns).

Units 2–3 pivot to **"Get around in a city"**: family ("Big Family"), travel, possessive adjectives ("mi", "tu"), locations, and phrases, building toward simple functional sentences a traveler or new speaker would need. The pattern across the whole course is thematic units (each grouping 4–7 lessons around one topic — travel, food, education, family, conversation) rather than isolated grammar drills, and short review lessons close out each unit.

**Takeaway for Papaya:** open with greetings + a tiny grammar seed (not gender-focused for us, since we're going grammar-free per §4) and family, then widen outward by daily-life topic (home, food, city/school), exactly the "expanding radius from the child's own world" structure Papaya should follow.

### 2.2 Duolingo ABC (ages 3–8, literacy app)

Duolingo ABC sequences by **foundational literacy skill**, not vocabulary theme: letters and letter sounds one at a time → phonics → sight words → simple stories, in bite-sized lessons (700+) mapped to a visual "world" of buildings the child unlocks. It is aligned to Common Core and National Reading Panel guidance, and pairs every new sound/word with **multi-sensory interaction** — tracing, drag-and-drop, read-along narration, repeat-aloud.

**Takeaway for Papaya:** since our audience (5–10) includes pre-readers, lessons should be introduced primarily through **audio + picture**, with text as reinforcement rather than the primary channel, exactly as Duolingo ABC treats early sight-words.

### 2.3 Lingokids

Lingokids organizes its whole curriculum as **10 units × 6 topics = 60 topics**, and each topic teaches **5–10 key vocabulary words**, with proficiency organized into levels (e.g., an "A1" beginner path with several hundred short activities). Topics span daily life, social-emotional skills, and academic basics (numbers, letters) rather than pure grammar.

**Takeaway for Papaya:** Lingokids' "10 units, several topics each, 5–10 words per topic" shape is almost exactly the deliverable format requested in §6 below — validating a 10-unit-first-world / 5–8-words-per-lesson structure as an industry-tested size for this age group.

## 3. Dialect choice: Latin American / Colombian Spanish, not Castilian

Papaya should teach **Latin American Spanish with Colombian household vocabulary**, for one simple reason: the dad is Colombian, and the whole point is the kid being able to talk to him and to grandparents. Concretely:

| Concept | Spain (Castilian) | Latin America / Colombia | Papaya's choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| car | coche | **carro** | carro |
| computer | ordenador | **computador** (Colombia specifically favors the masculine *computador*, vs. the feminine *computadora* used in Mexico and other LATAM countries) | computador |
| juice | zumo | **jugo** | jugo |
| "you-all" grammar | **vosotros** (informal plural) exists as a distinct form | **ustedes** covers both formal and informal plural — vosotros is not used and can be skipped entirely | ustedes-only; no vosotros forms anywhere in the app |
| stroller/cart (false-friend risk) | el coche = stroller | el coche = taxi/old wagon in some countries; carro/carrito used for stroller/cart in most of LATAM | avoid "coche" entirely to prevent confusion |

The **vosotros vs. ustedes** choice is the single highest-leverage grammar decision: essentially every Latin American Spanish course skips vosotros conjugations entirely, since it is not used in any Latin American country and Papaya's target speakers (Colombian father, Colombian grandparents) would never use it. Papaya should never surface vosotros forms.

### 3.1 Colombian affectionate/family language to include

- **mijo / mija** — contraction of "mi hijo/mi hija," used warmly by parents and grandparents (also sometimes used to soften a scolding — context matters, but for Papaya it should appear only in its warm, endearing sense).
- **mi amor** — "my love," extremely common from a Colombian parent to a child, not just romantic.
- **un abrazo / un beso** — "a hug / a kiss," commonly said and requested in families.
- **gordo/gorda** as a term of endearment is common across Latin America (including Colombia) but is body-focused and easy to misuse in an app aimed at kids — **excluded** from Papaya.
- **parcero/parcera** ("buddy/dude") is genuine Colombian slang but is peer/youth slang, not a parent-to-young-child register — **excluded**, consistent with the brief's instinct.
- Diminutives (**-ito/-ita**, e.g., "chiquito," "Andrecito") are a real and pervasive way Colombian families express affection, and are worth reflecting in tone/copywriting (mascot lines, celebrations) even where they aren't taught as standalone vocabulary items.

## 4. Grammar-free sequencing: chunks, not rules

Developmental research is consistent: **children ages 4–8 are not ready for explicit grammar rules** — the prefrontal-cortex machinery for abstract rule systems isn't there yet, and this age group learns through story, repetition, song, movement, and emotional engagement rather than rule explanation. Gender marking is a good test case: Spanish-speaking children as young as 34–42 months already use gender-marked articles correctly in speech *without ever being taught the rule*, purely from repeated exposure; explicit noticing of the *pattern* (e.g., "words ending in -a are usually 'la'") doesn't become useful until roughly ages 6–8, and even then it should stay light-touch (pointing it out during a story, not a grammar lesson).

**Implication for Papaya:**

- **el/la and plurals should never be taught as a rule.** Every noun item should simply always appear with its correct article baked into the audio and label from lesson one (e.g., teach "**el perro**," not "perro" + a separate gender lecture). By age 7–8, once a child has seen hundreds of "el ___" / "la ___" pairs, the app can start *gently surfacing* the pattern (e.g., a bonus mini-game sorting words into an "el" bucket and a "la" bucket) — but this is optional polish for later units/worlds, not a v1 requirement.
- **Plurals** should be introduced the same way — as whole learned chunks ("los perros," "las galletas") tied to a picture of *more than one* thing, not as "add -s" instruction.
- **Sentence frames** are the right tool for building beyond single words without teaching grammar: fixed, memorized openers that a child fills in with vocabulary they already know:
  - **"Yo quiero ___"** (I want ___) — for needs and requests.
  - **"Me gusta ___"** (I like ___) — for preference/opinion.
  - **"Tengo ___"** (I have ___ / I feel ___, e.g. "tengo hambre") — one of the highest-value frames since *tener* covers both possession and physical states in Spanish; teach it as a fixed chunk rather than explaining the irregular verb *tener*.
  - Every time the frame is used in a real interaction (wanting a snack, saying they like something), that's a natural moment to reuse it — reinforcing the chunk instead of introducing new grammar.
- Sentence frames should be introduced only *after* the learner has a starter set of nouns to plug into them (roughly by unit 5 in the curriculum below), never before.

## 5. Exercise types for pre-readers and early readers (ages 5–10)

| Exercise type | Needs audio? | Needs reading? | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Picture → word (multiple choice)** | Optional (can show word text + picture options) | Light | New nouns; the single most-used pattern across kids' apps |
| **Listen-and-tap** (hear the Spanish word, tap the matching picture) | **Required** | None | Pre-readers; the core mechanic recommended for non-readers since it needs zero text |
| **Match pairs** (Spanish word/picture ↔ English word/picture, memory-style) | Optional | Light–moderate | Reinforcement/review lessons |
| **Order the words** (drag 2–3 word chunks into a simple sentence, e.g. "yo" + "quiero" + "agua") | Optional | Moderate (needs word recognition, not full reading) | Sentence-frame lessons (unit 5+) |
| **TPR — Total Physical Response** (hear a command, perform/tap the matching action or have the on-screen hero perform it) | **Required** | None | Commands unit and action verbs; especially well-suited to this age group |
| **Songs / chants** | **Required** | None | Numbers, greetings, routines — anything naturally rhythmic; strong for retention and mood |

**Why TPR matters specifically for Papaya:** TPR was developed by James Asher by observing that parent–child interaction is dominated by commands followed by physical responses, and that this is how first-language learners absorb language with almost no stress or performance pressure. Because the child doesn't have to *speak* to prove they understood — just act — TPR is ideal for the "parent commands" vocabulary set (ven aquí, lávate las manos, siéntate, a comer) that Papaya specifically wants to teach, and it doubles as a battle-mechanic-compatible exercise: "the hero hears a command and must act it out/tap the right response before the timer runs out" fits Mundo-Quest/Papaya-style speed-quiz mechanics directly.

**Practical exercise-type guidance for pre-readers (ages 5–6):** lean almost entirely on listen-and-tap and TPR, since both require zero reading. **For early readers (ages 7–10):** picture-to-word, match-pairs, and order-the-words become viable, but should always keep audio available as a tap-to-hear option, since reading fluency in this age band varies widely even in the child's native language.

## 6. Deliverable: Papaya Curriculum — World 1

**Structure:** 10 units → 4–6 lessons per unit → 5–8 target items per lesson. Dialect: Latin American Spanish with Colombian household vocabulary; no *vosotros*; every noun's article is baked into its Spanish text where natural. **Total items: 351.**

Sequencing follows §1–§5: greetings/family/feelings first (highest daily-use payoff), then body/routine (unlocks parent commands), then concrete noun sets (colors, numbers, food, animals, clothes, home, school) matched to picture-based exercises, and only in the later units do sentence frames, question words, and full parent-command phrases appear, once the child has enough vocabulary to plug into them.

### 6.2 Notes for the engineer converting this to data files

- The JSON block above is close to production-ready; each lesson can become one `lesson.json`/DB row with an `items` array of `{es, en, emoji}` objects.
- Every noun carries its article (`el`/`la`) directly in the `es` field rather than as separate metadata — this matches the "teach gender as part of the word, never as a rule" guidance in §4. If Papaya later wants an el/la sorting mini-game, add a derived `gender` field programmatically from the leading article rather than re-authoring content.
- Items containing `___` are sentence-frame items and should render as fill-in-the-blank exercises (order-the-words or picture-to-blank), not simple flashcards — they depend on vocabulary already taught earlier in the same or prior units.
- Emoji are a placeholder for real art/photos; a few (numbers 11+, "el óvalo", "la estrella de mar") are approximations and should be swapped for custom illustrations before ship — flagged here so they're not missed.
- Recommended exercise-type mapping per §5: units 1–2 and the "commands" lessons (2.5, 8.5) → listen-and-tap + TPR; units 3–9 concrete-noun lessons → picture-to-word + match-pairs; unit 10 and the "frase" lessons (3.5, 5.5, 7.5) → order-the-words, introduced only after the child has cleared the preceding vocabulary-heavy units.
